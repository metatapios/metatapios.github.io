Research

I am a theoretical–computational astrophysicist. I build analytic and semi-analytic models and push them into the strongly non-linear regime with numerical experiments on supercomputers, using codes parallelised on many CPUs and GPUs.

My work sits in high-energy astrophysics and revolves around three ingredients:

  1. compact objects – neutron stars and black holes – and their birth in catastrophic events such as binary neutron star (BNS) mergers and the collapse of massive stars;
  2. strong shocks in collimated, magnetised, relativistic plasma outflows – jets;
  3. the acceleration of particles at those shocks up to TeV–PeV energies – cosmic rays – and the photons and neutrinos they produce.

These ingredients appear both in quasi-steady systems that live for decades or centuries, like supernova remnants and microquasars, and in transients lasting from a fraction of a minute to tens of days, like gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) and kilonovae. My main tools are the relativistic MHD code PLUTO and its GPU version gPLUTO, the moving-mesh code AREPO, and my own post-processing and radiative codes in Python and C (see Data & Code).

Current research

The GW–EM delay and shock breakout in neutron star mergers

In GW170817 the gamma-ray burst arrived ≈1.74 s after the gravitational-wave signal. Because the jet may only be launched after the hypermassive neutron star left by the merger collapses into a black hole, this delay indirectly probes the collapse time and the equation of state of nuclear matter. With R. Ciolfi and A. Pavan, I run 2D and 3D relativistic MHD simulations with PLUTO, initialised from realistic post-merger environments taken from general-relativistic MHD merger simulations and including the fast outer tail of the ejecta, which controls the optical depth and the breakout. Varying the two key parameters – jet launch time and jet power – I follow the shock up to the photosphere with a relativistic breakout condition, accounting for geometry, light-travel time and relativistic beaming.

The delay turns out to depend only weakly on viewing angle and ejecta opacity when the breakout happens near the outer edge of the ejecta, and a late-launched, moderate-luminosity jet best reproduces GW170817. I also compute bolometric shock-breakout light curves with a tomographic reconstruction over equal-arrival-time surfaces including relativistic Doppler effects.

Next: an end-to-end semi-analytic model calibrated on simulations – jet–cocoon shock shape, oblique breakout and multi-frequency light curves (afterglow included) versus viewing angle – to extract quasi-universal scaling relations that can serve as templates for future joint GW–EM detections.

Pais, Ciolfi & Pavan 2026, A&A

Jets in the aftermath of binary neutron star mergers

At the Hebrew University, with T. Piran, Y. Lyubarsky, K. Kiuchi and M. Shibata, I followed Poynting-flux-dominated jets through the ejecta of a one-second-long GR-MHD, neutrino-radiation merger simulation. Balancing the ejecta pressure against the jet magnetic pressure gives an approximately parabolic, collimated jet whose opening angle depends on its luminosity. A low-density polar cavity, carved by angular-momentum conservation after the merger, turns out to be essential for the jet to emerge – otherwise it is choked by the dense, neutron-rich material.

We then injected jets with different opening angles, luminosities and launch times (0.1 s and 1 s, the latter consistent with GW170817) into post-merger snapshots, deriving the minimum engine duration needed for breakout. Comparing synthetic afterglow light curves with the radio data of GW170817 favours a jet of ~3×1049 erg, active for 0.5 s, with a 5°–7° opening angle and launched 1 s after merger – an upper limit on the jet energy – and yields a generalised degeneracy relation between afterglow parameters and viewing angle.

Pais et al. 2023, ApJL · Pais et al. 2024, ApJ

Choked jets, collapsars and low-luminosity GRBs

Broad absorption lines in the early spectra of many stripped-envelope (Type Ib/c) supernovae require fast material that becomes optically thin very quickly – naturally explained by the cocoon of a jet that, in most cases, never makes it out of the star. With T. Piran and E. Nakar I showed that jets choked near the stellar edge drive outflows with an energy–velocity tail spanning several orders of magnitude, whose energy depends only on the ratio between the shocked-cocoon volume and the stellar volume, for a wide range of jet opening angles and luminosities and independently of the progenitor. This quasi-universal relation lets spectroscopic campaigns infer jet properties even when no GRB is seen. I also explore low-luminosity GRBs as the breakout of a mildly relativistic cocoon from stars with extended envelopes, computing the phase velocity of the breakout along the stellar surface and the resulting optical light curve.

Pais, Piran & Nakar 2023, MNRAS

Choked jets as hidden neutrino sources

The origin of the astrophysical high-energy neutrino flux is still unclear: only part of it is attributed to a sub-dominant Galactic component and to NGC 1068, and neither ANTARES nor IceCube found significant associations with GRBs – pointing to sources that are dark in gamma rays. Jets choked inside massive stellar envelopes are ideal candidates: gamma rays are trapped by the huge optical depth while neutrinos escape almost unattenuated. The cocoon-driven shock can still break out of the star with an X-ray/UV flash followed by day-long UV–optical emission, within reach of ULTRASAT or wide-field facilities such as MezzoCielo.

With S. Celli, A. Zegarelli and E. Peretti, and now within KM3NeT, I study when jets remain choked in blue and red supergiants and whether their shocks can accelerate protons that produce neutrinos through photo-hadronic interactions, using 3D relativistic MHD simulations of massive stars initialised from 3D GR-MHD collapsar simulations. A key question is whether strongly magnetised jets can still drive collisionless shocks – needed for particle acceleration – in extremely dense, radiation-dominated environments.

Pais et al. 2025, ICRC · Celli et al. 2025, HEPRO IX

Jets, kilonovae and r-process nucleosynthesis

GW170817 confirmed that neutron star mergers power both kilonovae – thermal transients from the radioactive decay of r-process elements – and relativistic jets. A framework linking the merger, the jet propagation and the nucleosynthesis is still missing. I aim to determine how a jet reshapes post-merger nucleosynthesis (e.g. through photodissociation that suppresses proton capture) by following the electron fraction Ye along the ejecta. The pipeline chains GR-MHD merger simulations (Spritz), jet–ejecta interaction with PLUTO, Lagrangian tracers post-processed with SkyNet, and kilonova light curves with xKN – from GW170817-like systems with different equations of state and injected jets, to jets launched self-consistently after the delayed collapse to a black hole – quantifying the role of neutrino transport (leakage vs. M1).

Ongoing – subject of my MSCA proposal KiloJets (Seal of Excellence 2025).

Galactic PeVatrons: microquasars

Identifying the Galactic accelerators that reach PeV energies is one of the open questions of high-energy astrophysics. Microquasars are a promising class, as shown by the very-high-energy gamma rays along the jets of SS 433. With E. Amato, G. Morlino and E. Peretti (INAF – Arcetri) I run MHD simulations with PLUTO to map the jet dynamics and the structure of their shocks – Mach disks and recollimation shocks. On the code side, I am extending PLUTO's Lagrangian cosmic-ray particle module to the GPU version for non-Cartesian, non-uniform grids (with A. Mignone), and developing a new cosmic-ray fluid module dynamically coupled to the gas to model diffusive shock acceleration and the resulting non-thermal emission. The goal: quantify the contribution of microquasars to Galactic PeVatrons and to their gamma-ray and neutrino emission.

Gravitational waves from jets

Relativistic jets should produce a gravitational-wave memory signal while they accelerate, so far computed analytically only for unstructured, instantaneously accelerated jets. I use hydrodynamic simulations of structured jets from BNS mergers and collapsars to compute the GW signal along the whole jet evolution, providing a physically motivated template for the pulse associated with the launch phase, following the inspiral signal.

Past research

Cosmic-ray acceleration at supernova remnant shocks

During my PhD with C. Pfrommer (HITS and AIP) I studied supernova remnants (SNRs), prime candidates for accelerating cosmic rays (CRs) up to near-PeV energies. The CR microphysics (Larmor radii of ~1011–1012 cm) and the SNR dynamics (0.1–10 pc) are separated by many orders of magnitude, so I used a fluid description with sub-grid CR transport in the moving-mesh MHD code AREPO: CRs are injected at shocks found on the fly and evolved with an advection–diffusion equation. Since kinetic simulations show that the acceleration efficiency depends on the angle between the magnetic field and the shock normal, I implemented obliquity-dependent CR acceleration and explored it in uniform and turbulent magnetic fields.

We then produced synthetic TeV gamma-ray maps of shell-type and bilateral SNRs in hadronic and lepto-hadronic scenarios, mimicking the PSF and counting noise of Cherenkov telescopes like H.E.S.S., and used them to constrain the coherence length of the interstellar magnetic field. We showed for the first time that a composite interstellar medium, with thousands of small dense clumps interacting with the remnant shock, is needed to reproduce the observed morphology, spectrum and flux – while density fluctuations alone cannot explain the azimuthal TeV variations without obliquity-dependent acceleration.

Pais et al. 2018 · Pais et al. 2020a · Pais & Pfrommer 2020b · Winner et al. 2020 · PhD thesis
